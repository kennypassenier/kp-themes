Form fields

.kp-field around every control a form has, .kp-fieldset for a group, .kp-form with its error summary, and .kp-error for the page a form ends on when it cannot go on. Every state a field can be in, and the two extremes that break one: a label too long for its column and a value too long for its box.

Text, email, number

Look at: three inputs of three types must look like one family: the same height, the same border, the same ground, and the label above each at the same distance. Click into each and Tab between them — the focus ring must be visible against the input's own ground and the page's, and it must not be the border merely changing colour. The placeholder in the empty one must read as a hint, clearly lighter than a typed value, yet still legible. The number input's spin buttons are the browser's; check that they do not sit on a white square in a dark theme.

Textarea and select

Look at: the textarea is .kp-field__input--multiline: it must be taller than a single-line input, it must resize vertically only — drag its corner sideways and nothing should happen — and its text must start at the same inset as the inputs above. Open the select: in a browser that allows it the list wears the theme (the popover surface, a soft highlight, a check on the chosen option); in one that does not, the native list appears, and that is the browser's, not a fault. Closed, the select must be the same height as a text input.

Checkbox and radio

Look at: the box comes before its label and shares its baseline. Tick and untick: the checked mark must be visible in every theme — in a theme whose primary colour is its ink, a checked box that looks unchecked is the known fault. The radio group is a .kp-fieldset: the legend reads as the question, the options as answers, and clicking a label must select its radio. The second group asks for data-kp-layout="inline"; its options sit in a row and wrap. There is no switch in the package yet — a toggle is a checkbox here, and the switch is a concept on its own page for now.

Help and required

Look at: the help text sits under the input, smaller and quieter than the label, but must still pass contrast on the page's ground — check it with the Devtools colour tool in the palest and the darkest theme. The required marker is a star and the word: the star in the alarm colour, the word muted. A theme may swap the glyph, never drop the word. The optional field beside it must not look any less important for having no marker.

Invalid, with its message

Look at: every invalid field says what is wrong in words, under the control, in the alarm colour — and the colour must pass contrast on the ground, because the words are the message. The text input's border turns to the alarm colour; the checkbox, which has no border, gets an outline instead. The radio group carries aria-invalid on the group and gets one outline around the whole group — one wrong answer, one mark — while the radios inside it stay unframed. In a theme whose register repaints the input's border, the invalid border must still win over it; every theme answers that state now. The help text must survive beside the error rather than be replaced by it. Compare with the valid field at the end: the difference must be visible without colour — the message is what carries it.

Disabled and read-only

Look at: these two are different promises and must not look the same. Disabled cannot be used and says so: the pointer shows it is refused and the value is still readable. Read-only can be focused, selected and copied, but not changed — it should look like a value on a record, not like a broken input. The package fades the disabled field and gives the read-only one a dashed boundary on the muted ground at full ink. If the read-only field looks exactly like an editable one in some theme, a reviewer will type into it and think the page froze: that is the fault to watch for. The disabled checkbox and select must follow the same rule as the text input.

Error summary

Look at: the first form shows the summary as js/forms.js writes it after a failed submit: a boundary in the alarm colour, a tinted ground, a sentence saying how many fields are wrong, and one link per field that moves focus to it. The tint must stay a tint — in a dark theme it must not become a red slab that swallows the links. The second form is live: press Report with both fields empty, and the summary must appear and take focus, with a visible focus ring around it; then fill one field and Tab away, and its message must clear without a second submit.

Extremes

Look at: the label is 120 characters: it must wrap onto as many lines as it needs and stay aligned to the input's left edge — never be cut, never push the field wider than its column. The input holds a 300-character value on one line: it must scroll inside itself, keep its height, and keep its padding on both ends when you move the caret to the end. The textarea holds the same 300 characters and must wrap them at word boundaries inside its padding. The last input holds an identifier with nowhere to break; it too must stay inside its box.

A form in a narrow pane

Look at: drag the pane's right edge until it is as narrow as it goes. Every field must shrink with it — no input may stick out past the dashed edge, and no label may overlap its neighbour. The checkbox's label and its error must wrap under the box rather than into a column one word wide. The buttons at the bottom must wrap onto two lines rather than overflow. The gap between fields must stay the same at every width: it is the form's, not the pane's.

Error page

Look at: .kp-error is the page a form lands on when the server gave up. The heading takes the alarm colour, the explanation stays on the ordinary ground in the ordinary ink — a whole page in red is a wall, not a message. The column must be narrow enough to read (about sixty characters) and centred. The way back must be a real button, not a sentence telling you to press Back.
