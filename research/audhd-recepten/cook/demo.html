Cook mode

The part of the recipe app that carries someone through a dish without asking them to hold the sequence in their head. It starts with one small step (gather the things), shows one cooking step at a time, keeps every timer running where it can be seen, and asks for attention only when something is done. The sample dish is Spaghetti with tomato sauce and meatballs, where the pasta and the sauce run at the same time. The timers here run fast (1 minute lasts 3 seconds) so you can watch them end; Real time is one button away. All data is mock.

Begin: gather everything first

Pressing Begin does not start step 1; it opens a short checklist of what to take out, and ticked items sink so only what is left stays on top.

Expected:

  • Before Begin there is one card and one big button, nothing else to decide. Press Begin: the checklist loads (a short busy card), then opens under the card in three groups.
  • Tick a row: it sinks to the bottom of its group, struck through, and the group counter and the bar count up. Check all comes with Uncheck all.
  • Start cooking is off until everything is ticked and says how many are left; Skip, start anyway is always there, because a missing onion must never block dinner.
  • Why it helps: the first action is tiny and has no wrong answer (task initiation), the tick gives instant feedback, and gathering first means nothing is missed halfway through a step (working memory).
  • The state buttons show the list loading (busy card), empty and failed, each at the list’s full height.

Formal: the Begin card is one plain card with a single primary button; the checklist rows have a clear tick target on the whole row, and a ticked row is greyed and struck through at the bottom of its group.

Cook mode: one step at a time

Nine steps on the counter, one at a time and large, with what the step needs and a timer button; started timers keep running in the list beside it while you go on to the next step.

Expected:

  • Step 1 shows Step 1 of 9, the progress bar, the screen line (Screen stays on where the browser can), Read aloud, the step, what it needs, a timer button and a note saying which track it belongs to (Pasta or Sauce).
  • Press Start a timer · 10 min on step 1: a Water timer appears in the list and keeps counting. Go on with Next: steps 3, 4 and 5 start Onion, Sauce and Pasta, so Water, Sauce and Pasta run side by side while you stir and taste.
  • When a timer ends its card turns to the done plate with a chime, and the band above the step says which one and offers Go to step N for what comes next: nothing has to be remembered.
  • Arrow keys, Space, Home and End move between steps; a swipe does the same on a touch screen. The buttons never move, whatever the step’s length.
  • Why it helps: one thing on screen at a time (no scanning a recipe), the running timers are always visible (time blindness), the screen stays on with messy hands, and the end of a timer asks for attention once, kindly.
  • The parallel tracks are shown with what the package has today: the track is named in each step’s note and the timers carry the track names. The real track option is under Component gaps in the README.

Formal: the step is large dark text on the white card, the two navigation buttons sit at the foot in fixed places, and the timer cards on the right show the time left in figures that do not jump as they count.

Running timers: Pasta and Sauce together

Named timers run side by side, each with its own bar, pause and +1 min; when one ends, a band above them names it and offers the one thing to do.

Expected:

  • Press Start both: Pasta (9 min) and Sauce (10 min) appear as two cards with the time left, a state word, a bar, Pause, +1 min and Remove. Nothing in a card changes size as the clock runs.
  • When one ends, its card becomes the done plate (“Pasta is done”) with one big Stop, the sound plays and the band above says Pasta is done with Stop Pasta. The Sauce keeps counting.
  • Try the end starts a 5 second Pasta so you can hear the sound choices (chime, bell, buzzer, off), the voice and the repeat time without waiting.
  • Why it helps: the sound and the voice give the time sense that is missing, the band is one place to look instead of hunting for a ringing card, and a timer pressed twice does not start a second one.
  • Clear all removes every timer and shows the empty list at its full height; the list itself has no loading or failed state (see Component gaps).

Formal: two white cards with figures of one width and a thin navy progress bar; the done plate is the only coloured card, and the attention band above is the one amber line.

Time at a glance: the shrinking disc

The same Pasta and Sauce as a coloured disc that grows smaller as time goes, so the time left is seen without reading a number; it follows the timers you start in the list.

Expected:

  • Two discs, Pasta · 27 s and Sauce · 30 s in this fast demo (9 and 10 minutes in real time), each a sector of the dial, with Start and Reset. Press Start both: the edges turn back towards twelve o’clock; nothing ticks on the face.
  • Start a Pasta or Sauce timer in the two sections above and its disc starts with it, pauses with it and ends with it; at the end the face breathes three times and says Time is up.
  • The default is a 60-minute dial, the classic Time Timer: a 9-minute pasta is a sector of 54°, so two timers of different length can be compared at a glance (in this fast demo the dial is 3 minutes). Own length makes each disc start full whatever its length. Disabled shows the discs with their buttons off, for a recipe that has not loaded.
  • Why it helps: “how long is left” becomes an amount you can see from across the kitchen, which is how time is felt when numbers do not register; and nothing on the face flickers.

Formal: the disc is a flat navy sector on a pale face with twelve small marks round the rim, the name and length centred under it, and Start in the primary colour next to a quiet Reset.

On a phone: calm mode and the states

The same cook mode at phone width, with the switch that turns movement and effects off for a day with no energy, and the loading, no-steps and failed states of the recipe.

Expected:

  • The frame is 390 px wide. The Calm mode switch sits above the step; turn it on and the step change, the timers’ arrival and the effects stop moving (the timers still count and still ring).
  • The three groups say how far calm goes: Transitions only, Transitions and effects (default) or Everything, and whether a loading part breathes slowly or holds still.
  • Loading lays the busy card over the step at its full height; No steps says so in the theme’s empty plate; Failed says what happened in an alert in the step’s place. The footprint never shrinks.
  • Why it helps: on a low-energy or overloaded day movement itself is tiring, so calm is one switch, not a hunt through settings, and an empty day is quiet rather than failed.

Formal: with calm on, nothing glides or glows: steps change at once and the loading card holds its shape; the switch itself shows on or off in plain words.
