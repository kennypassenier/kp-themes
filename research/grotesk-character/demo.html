What grotesk is

Grotesk is the timetable: information set on a grid, running on time. White paper, black type and one red, a twelve-column grid, headings in Archivo heavy and tight, flush left. "Twelve Columns" (2026-09-08) was the Swiss poster; your later picks carry it into the station: the transit board four times, the transit map, the flap board, Timetable and Express, the ticker, the train that passes.

The two meet in one idea: everything is set, and runs on time, and the anchor is how it runs: a second, red plate falls into register on the black one. A part stands in its column under a rule. What arrives is set along its line from the start and stops flush; it moves at an even pace and stops dead, the way the Swiss station clock does, and a loop dwells at its station before it goes again. What is pointed at has its red plate fall into register on it; what is pressed is held in register; while something waits, its proof is out of register until it registers; a new value is re-registered; a warning is indexed; what opens and what leaves is printed with its red plate falling in, and released as the same fall played backwards.
